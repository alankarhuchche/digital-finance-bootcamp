# R5. Economic crime risks and bank controls for stablecoin flows

Research input for the Stablecoins topic: the financial crime controls journey and the unhosted or flagged wallet teaching scenario. This is evidence for editors, not site copy.

- Research window: 17 to 18 November 2026. The model's training data ends around June 2026. Every later fact below comes from a live source, and anything that could not be confirmed is labelled unverified.
- "Excerpt only" means the item was seen in search-engine results but the page itself was not read. Those rows carry Low or Med confidence.
- Vendor figures (Chainalysis, TRM Labs, Elliptic, Bitquery) are labelled as vendor estimates. Company statements (Tether, Bybit, Binance) are self-reported.
- Scope rule: typologies are described only at control-design level.

## 1. Editor summary

1. In the UK, unhosted wallets are handled with risk-based controls, not a ban. Reg 64G lets a cryptoasset business ask its own customer for data and bars release if requested data is not received. JMLSG expects proof of control in higher-risk cases. The EU goes further: above EUR 1,000 an ownership/control assessment is mandatory. (AML-19, 22, 33, 35)
2. A change after June 2026: since 30 June 2026 the UK Travel Rule threshold has been £800 instead of EUR 1,000 (SI 2026/621). JMLSG Annex 22-I still says EUR 1,000. New enhanced due diligence (EDD) for crypto correspondent relationships starts on 1 Feb 2027. The FSMA cryptoasset regime starts on 25 Oct 2027. (AML-17, 32)
3. Expect counterparties to comply with the Travel Rule unevenly. FATF (July 2026) found that 83% of surveyed jurisdictions have passed the law, but 60% of those have taken no Travel Rule supervisory or enforcement action. (AML-04, 05)
4. FATF calls peer-to-peer (P2P) transfers through unhosted wallets a "structural gap", because no obliged entity files reports on them. 88% of responding jurisdictions rate P2P as high risk, but only 23% measure it. (AML-07, 08)
5. Issuer freezes are not a universal backstop. FATF warns that obliged entities "may be unable to rely" on them. Freeze-resistant tokens exist: FinCEN calls A7A5 a "non-freezable, bridging asset". (AML-09, 45)
6. Speed decides whether a freeze works. In the October 2026 Binance theft, Tether froze about 1.18bn USDT 37 minutes after it arrived, but roughly USD 0.7 to 0.8bn of stablecoins had already been swapped and about USD 3.25bn in native assets stayed out of reach (vendor data). After the Bybit theft, 3.84% had been frozen two months later. (AML-49, 51, 52)
7. The headline illicit-volume figures are minimums driven by sanctions flows. Chainalysis reports at least USD 154bn in 2025, 84% of it in stablecoins, and below 1% of attributed on-chain volume. TRM reports USD 158bn, or 1.2%. The 84% is not the share of stablecoin activity that is criminal. (AML-61, 62, 74)
8. A7A5 is the best-dated sanctions case. US, UK and EU designations followed in Aug to Oct 2025, and the Grinex exchange halted in Apr 2026. The EU banned transactions with Russia-based crypto providers from 24 May 2026, and the US designated the A7 network a transnational criminal organisation on 1 Oct 2026. FinCEN observed the token's supply moving into unhosted wallets. (AML-39 to 46)
9. OFSI (the UK sanctions authority) sets out the design constraints. A recipient cannot reject an inbound transfer. Firms should screen 3 to 5 hops at minimum, and a new wallet receiving a large transfer is a red flag. OFSI judged it "almost certain" that UK crypto firms under-report suspected sanctions breaches. (AML-24 to 28)
10. Analytics output is probabilistic and changes over time. Courts have admitted vendor tracing evidence (Bitcoin Fog, affirmed on appeal 25 Sep 2026), while witnesses conceded no peer review and an unknown error rate. A new address that scores "low risk" has no evidence against it, which is not the same as good evidence. (AML-71, 76)
11. Ownership proofs show who controlled a key at one moment. They do not prove beneficial ownership or that a supplier is legitimate, and a supplier's wallet belongs to a third party, not the bank's customer. (AML-35, 77)
12. UK escalation route: a suspicious activity report (SAR) to the National Crime Agency (NCA), and a defence against money laundering (DAML) before any prohibited act. The DAML notice period is 7 working days, followed by a 31-day moratorium if consent is refused. Report to OFSI where a designated person is suspected. DAML timelines only work if the bank holds the payment. (AML-26, 30)
13. Compare issuer models rather than ranking them. Tether self-reports broad freeze cooperation. Circle's policy is reported to be narrower. A US rule proposed under the GENIUS Act would make block and freeze capability mandatory for US issuers. (AML-66, 69, 70)
14. The largest losses were operational. At both Bybit and Binance, attackers compromised signing infrastructure and people, not the cryptography. Researchers counted 270M address-poisoning attempts that caused at least USD 83.8m in losses. (AML-47, 50, 72, 73)
15. Unverified: the EU Commission's self-hosted wallet report (due 1 Jul 2026), a JMLSG update for the £800 threshold, the final GENIUS Act rule, the ruling on Storm's acquittal motion, the Pertsev appeal, the closure of the T3 unit, and the exact wording of AMLR Art 79 and 90. FATF's own web pages returned HTTP 403.

## 3. Control journey

Roles: **Bank** (payer's or payee's bank, or a bank acting as a cryptoasset business), **VASP** (exchange, custodian or payment provider), **Issuer**, **Analytics** (blockchain analytics provider, which supplies data while the bank or VASP owns the decision), **LE** (law enforcement and authorities: NCA, OFSI, FCA, FBI and others).

### 3.1 Pre-execution

| Control | What it catches | What it misses | Who performs it |
|---|---|---|---|
| KYC/KYB and payment purpose (contract, invoice, why stablecoin, why this address) | Named sanctioned or unknown parties, implausible business rationale, geography mismatch | Who actually controls the address. Nested or offshore VASPs posing as ordinary clients (AML-58) | Bank, VASP |
| Wallet attribution: hosted or unhosted, which VASP, which jurisdiction (AML-23) | The counterparty VASP, its registration and Travel Rule capability | Unlabelled addresses, misattribution, addresses inside nested services | Bank or VASP using Analytics data and Travel Rule directories |
| Address screening: list match plus exposure scoring, OFSI minimum 3 to 5 hops (AML-25) | Listed addresses, known clusters, direct and indirect exposure to sanctioned, mixer, theft and scam clusters | New addresses, clusters not yet attributed, labels added late, cross-chain hops, false positives from unsolicited small deposits (AML-28, 76) | Analytics data, Bank or VASP decision |
| Ownership or control proof where the bank's own customer claims the address (AML-22, 35) | Whether the customer holds the key (signed message, test transfer, attended verification) | Beneficial ownership, purpose, keys controlled for a third party. It does not apply cleanly to a supplier's wallet (AML-77) | Bank or VASP with the customer |
| Travel Rule data exchange with VASP counterparties (AML-16, 18) | Missing or mismatched names, which triggers delay or return. Names become available for sanctions screening | Unhosted P2P (AML-08), counterparties in jurisdictions without the rule (AML-05, 20), interoperability gaps (AML-21). For unhosted wallets the data is customer-asserted | Originator, intermediary and beneficiary VASPs |
| Allowlisted address book with change controls and confirmation through a separate channel | Substituted addresses from address poisoning or invoice redirection (AML-72) | A compromised approval workflow or approver | Bank operations with the corporate client |
| Limits, staged payments, detection of linked transfers, hold-before-release | Outsized first payments to new addresses, structuring | Patient low-value layering | Bank |

### 3.2 Execution

| Control | What it catches | What it misses | Who performs it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Re-screen at the moment of release | Designations or labels added since onboarding | Labels that arrive after broadcast | Bank or VASP via Analytics |
| Signing controls: policy engine, multi-party approval, segregated key custody, independent check of what is being signed | Unauthorised or altered withdrawals | Compromised signing interfaces, workstations or people (AML-47, 50, 73) | Bank or VASP custody function |
| Issuer contract-level blocking (denylist or allowlist) | Transfers from or to addresses already frozen | Addresses not yet frozen, freeze-resistant tokens, native assets (AML-09, 45) | Issuer |
| Handling of inbound funds from flagged sources | Funds from designated or high-risk sources that cannot be rejected on-chain (AML-26) | Funds credited automatically to omnibus wallets before screening completes | Bank or VASP |

### 3.3 Post-transaction

| Control | What it catches | What it misses | Who performs it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ongoing monitoring and periodic rescreening of counterparties and addresses | Later attribution, behaviour change, onward flows to risky services (AML-28) | Cash-outs via OTC or P2P, privacy tools. Because of the lag, funds are often gone | Bank or VASP with Analytics |
| Lookbacks after new designations | Past exposure to newly designated parties | Activity before designation, which is generally not a breach | Bank or VASP |
| Reporting: SAR or DAML to the NCA, breach and frozen-asset reports to OFSI, reports to the FCA of repeated Travel Rule failures (AML-18, 24, 30) | Produces the intelligence that drives freezes and investigations | Does not itself stop movement. DAML timelines clash with instant settlement unless funds are held | Bank or VASP; NCA, OFSI, FCA |
| Issuer freeze, burn and reissue on request from law enforcement (AML-66, 69) | Stablecoins still sitting in identified wallets | Value already swapped or bridged, DeFi collateral, differences between issuer policies (AML-52, 75) | Issuer with LE |
| Seizure and forfeiture (UK ECCTA powers; US forfeiture) (AML-31, 53, 55) | Assets at exchanges and custodians, and identified wallets | Assets in non-cooperating jurisdictions, self-custodied keys that cannot be found | LE and courts |
| Public-private rapid response (published addresses, issuer and analytics task forces) (AML-47, 50, 68) | Speeds up blocking across platforms | Depends on voluntary participation. The reported closure of the T3 unit shows how fragile this is | LE, Issuers, Analytics, VASPs |

## 4. Unhosted or flagged wallet scenario (UK law as at 18 Nov 2026)

**Set-up.** A corporate client asks its bank to pay a supplier in a USD stablecoin. The payment is commercially plausible. The beneficiary address is (a) unhosted, (b) newly created, or (c) flagged by analytics for sanctions or fraud exposure.

**First, fix the bank's role, because the obligations differ.**
- If the bank (or a subsidiary) sends the stablecoins from its own custody, it is acting as a cryptoasset exchange provider or custodian wallet provider. Part 7A Travel Rule duties apply (AML-15), and MLR registration applies until the FSMA regime starts on 25 Oct 2027 (AML-32). Whether an authorised bank also needs MLR registration is a strong inference from the MLRs, not re-verified in this pass.
- If the bank only pays fiat to a third-party VASP that sends the stablecoins, the Travel Rule and wallet duties sit with the VASP. The bank keeps its CDD, monitoring, sanctions and SAR duties, but may not see the destination address unless the client discloses it.

**What the bank can infer from chain data and analytics.**
- Whether the address is a contract or a plain key-controlled address, when it was first funded, its balance and its counterparties.
- Whether analytics attribute it to a VASP (hosted) or to nothing (presumed unhosted), and whether it appears on an official list. The OFSI Consolidated List includes some addresses (AML-24 source). OFAC also lists digital currency addresses on the SDN List (prior knowledge, not re-verified).
- Direct and indirect exposure to labelled clusters within the hop depth screened (AML-25).
- Pattern red flags such as a new wallet receiving a large transfer (AML-27).

**What the bank cannot infer.**
- Who controls the key, or on whose behalf. The address stays pseudonymous until off-chain evidence links it to a person.
- That a new address is safe. No history is no evidence in either direction (AML-76).
- That today's "low risk" score is right. Labels are proprietary, probabilistic and often added after the event (AML-28, 71).
- What happens next. Funds can move on within minutes (AML-51, 52).
- Whether the address is the genuine supplier's. Lookalike addresses and invoice redirection produce plausible but wrong addresses (AML-72).
- Anything about value that leaves the chain through P2P or OTC (AML-08, 49).

**What the bank may do (risk-based options, in rising order of escalation).**
1. Ask the client for the supplier's legal identity, contract or invoice, its reason for paying in stablecoins and its reason for using this address, and screen the supplier's name. Where the bank is the cryptoasset business, reg 64G supports this request (AML-19).
2. Prefer a hosted address at a registered or licensed VASP that can exchange Travel Rule data, or offer a fiat route instead.
3. Ask the supplier for proof of control, either a signed message or a small test transfer (AML-22, 35). Record it as evidence of key control only (AML-77).
4. Confirm the address with a known supplier contact through a separate channel, then allowlist it with change controls.
5. Stage the payment (test amount first), apply value limits, or hold it pending checks.
6. Decline the payment, or exit the relationship where the risk cannot be mitigated.
7. Keep the address under monitoring after payment and rescreen it.

**What the bank must do.**
- **Sanctions.** If the address is listed, or there is reasonable cause to suspect it is owned or controlled by a designated person, do not pay. Making funds available would breach the asset freeze. Freeze related assets the bank holds, report to OFSI, and file a SAR where money laundering is suspected. OFSI expects addresses, transaction hashes, values and the attribution rationale in reports (AML-24 to 28). That banks are "relevant firms" with OFSI reporting duties is prior knowledge, not re-verified.
- **Money laundering suspicion.** File a SAR with the NCA. If making the payment would itself be a prohibited act under POCA ss.327-329, request a DAML and wait until consent is deemed (7 working days with no refusal) or any 31-day moratorium ends (AML-30). Avoid tipping off (prior knowledge, not re-verified).
- **Travel Rule, where the bank is the cryptoasset business.** For an outbound transfer to an unhosted wallet, decide on a risk basis whether to request information. If requested information is not provided, do not make the transfer (AML-22). For a hosted beneficiary, send names and account identifiers, plus full originator data if the transfer is cross-border and at or above £800 (AML-16, 17). Document every decision. Respond "fully and without delay" to law-enforcement information requests (reg 64H).
- **Inbound leg.** Funds from a flagged address cannot be rejected on-chain. Restrict them, freeze where required, and report (AML-26).

**Variants for teaching.**
- Unhosted, long clean history, established client: proceeding with documented purpose, address confirmation and monitoring is defensible. Proof of control is good practice at higher values.
- Newly created address: there is no history to assess, and OFSI treats a large first transfer as a red flag. Expect EDD, proof of control and a staged payment before release.
- Sanctions flag: a listed address or direct exposure means stop and report. Indirect exposure needs analysis of the path and hop distance, with the rationale recorded either way (AML-25).
- Fraud flag (a scam cluster, a mule pattern, or supplier details changed recently): first treat it as possible invoice redirection or impersonation of the supplier. Stop, verify through known contacts, and consider a SAR.

**Other jurisdictions.**
- EU: the ownership or control assessment is mandatory above EUR 1,000 for self-hosted transfers, using EBA methods (AML-33, 35). AMLR Art 40 risk-mitigation duties start on 10 Jul 2027 (AML-37). Transactions with any Russia-established crypto provider have been banned since 24 May 2026 (AML-43).
- US: OFAC blocking duties extend to listed addresses (prior knowledge, not re-verified). FinCEN's A7 special measure and the GENIUS Act issuer rule were still proposals as at 18 Nov 2026 (AML-45, 70).
- FATF baseline: no global standard requires proof of ownership for unhosted wallets. FATF lists limits, EDD and analytics as observed measures (AML-13).

## 5. Myths, open questions and limits

**Myths to correct.**
- "Unhosted wallets are banned, or capped at EUR 1,000, in the EU." Neither the TFR nor the AMLR bans them. Above EUR 1,000 the CASP assesses ownership or control (AML-33, 37, 38). The cap appeared only in earlier drafts.
- "Travel Rule tools cover unhosted wallets." They exchange data between VASPs. For unhosted wallets the data comes from the bank's own customer (AML-19).
- "A low risk score means a clean wallet." See AML-28, 71 and 76.
- "Issuers can always freeze stolen or sanctioned stablecoins." See AML-09, 45, 52 and 75.
- "84% of crypto crime is in stablecoins, so stablecoins are mainly criminal." See AML-74. Vendors put illicit activity at roughly 1% of attributed volume.
- "Refusing a deposit removes the sanctions exposure." On-chain, a transfer cannot be refused (AML-26).
- "The Tornado Cash delisting means mixers are no longer an AML issue." Treasury remains concerned, OFSI lists mixer use as a red flag, and Storm's conviction stands with a retrial pending (AML-27, 59, 60).
- "A signed message proves who the supplier is." It proves only that someone controlled a key at one moment (AML-77).
- "Because ledgers are public, laundering is easy to stop." The first phase of Bybit laundering took about 10 days, and the Binance stablecoin swaps happened within minutes (AML-48, 51).

**Open questions (unverified as at 18 Nov 2026).**
- The EU Commission's report on self-hosted addresses, due 1 Jul 2026 under the TFR review clause, was not found (AML-36).
- Whether JMLSG has updated Annex 22-I for the £800 threshold (AML-17).
- Whether the GENIUS Act rule and FinCEN's A7 special measure have been finalised (AML-45, 70).
- The text of FATF's payment-transparency guidance for late 2026, and any R.15 alignment for VASPs (AML-02, 03).
- The FCA's financial crime rules under the 2027 FSMA regime were not researched in this pass.
- The ruling on Storm's acquittal motion, the Pertsev appeal, the T3 closure and the text of Circle's policy (AML-60, 68, 69).
- ECCTA 2023 also created DAML-related exemptions, for example for exiting relationships. Their thresholds and how they fit cryptoasset firms were not verified.
- The exact wording of AMLR Art 79 and Art 90 on EUR-Lex (AML-37, 38).

**Limits of the evidence.**
- Vendor figures are floors that rise over time. They depend on proprietary attribution and count all flows to sanctioned entities as illicit (AML-61, 62).
- FATF survey data is self-reported by jurisdictions (AML-04 to 07).
- Issuer and exchange freeze statistics are self-reported and unaudited (AML-49, 66, 67).
- Several 2026 items rest on secondary reporting because primary pages were blocked (section 6).
